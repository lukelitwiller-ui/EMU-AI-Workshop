The good news: nothing you build today needs to break that rule. You hand over the shape of your data - column names and one invented row - and it hands back instructions that run where your data already lives.

The tool you are using decides which light you are on. Same prompt, different agreement behind it, different rules. Activities 1, 2 and 3 assume the free public version - the strictest case.

Activity 4 is the one exception, and it is worth understanding why. Signed into your emu.edu account, Gemini Notebook runs under EMU’s Workspace for Education agreement rather than the public terms, so internal working documents are allowed there. The rule did not change - the container did. Check the top right corner and you will know which one you are in.

The method

The workflow overview

Treat it like a bright new intern who has never worked here. Capable, fast, eager, and completely ignorant of how your office runs. Whatever you would tell that person is your prompt.

  1. Name the task

    One sentence. What the job is, and what you want to come out the other end.

  2. Write the context a new employee would need

    This is the part that is easy to skip, and the part that decides whether this actually works. Where the information lives, what the columns are called and in what order, what the values mean, what a normal example looks like. It cannot see your screen and it will not ask.

  3. Hand it over as instructions - a first message, not a finished one

    Every rule, including what to leave alone. Every guardrail, including what it must never do. Then send it, knowing it is wrong somewhere. This is the opening of an iterative conversation, and it is extremely rare to get it perfect on the first try.

  4. Test it on an example where you already know the answer

    Not a real one. A made-up one, small, where you can count the correct result before you run it. If you cannot tell right from wrong, you cannot tell working from broken.

  5. Read the result like an editor, then go back to step three

    Tell it what it got wrong, in plain language, and paste the error if there is one. Then again. The loop is the method. Keep on iterating!

3 things to keep in mind

Your first message is a draft, and so is your understanding of your own process

In my example: I told it the report would arrive as a spreadsheet attachment. It built exactly that, flawlessly. There were no attachments. What I described was wrong.

It does not know when it is wrong, and it never sounds like it

In my example: a report had a line reading "Total Records: 4." My script decided 4 was a student ID and went looking for the student named 4. Nothing in the output looked wrong.

Small things can cause a script to break or not work as intended

In my example: mine looked for unread emails. If anyone opened one on their phone first, that student's data was gone - silently. The code was fine. My assumption was not.

The AI does not need to see a real person, or a real number, to be useful. Give it the shape of your data and an invented example.

Write the prompt

Write your own before you look at ours. Doing it yourself is the difference between leaving with a script and leaving able to build the next one.

Five moves

Open your sheet next to this and type straight into Gemini. Aim for complete, not elegant.

  1. Say what you want to happen

    Describe the outcome in your own words. You do not need to know what to ask for technically - that is the AI's job to figure out.

    "I want to stop chasing these people by hand" is a fine opening. Ask it what it would suggest. It may come back with a script, a formula, or something you had not considered - and any of those is a real answer.

    If you are stuck, start hereI use a Google Sheet to track people who each need to complete a requirement. I keep having to check who is overdue and write them the same email by hand. What could I do to make this less manual?

  2. Describe the sheet like it cannot see your screen, because it cannot

    Open your copy and look at it. What would a new employee need to know before they could work this list?

    The tab name, every column in the order they appear, what the values mean, and one made-up row so it can see the shape. Use a fake name.

    Column order matters. Get it wrong and it reads the wrong column - and the output still looks plausible.

  3. Write every rule, including the ones where the answer is "do nothing"

    Look at the Status and Due date columns, and work out at your table: how many different situations are in this sheet, and what should happen in each?

    There are five, and two of them are "leave this row alone." A rule that says do nothing is still a rule - leave it out and the AI invents something helpful instead. One of the five should not produce an email at all.

  4. Say what it must never do

    What is the worst thing this could do if it misunderstood you? Write the rule that prevents it.

    Say "drafts only, do not send anything." That line is the difference between a mistake and an incident.

    Then tell it who you are. "Explain what each part does, I don't write code" changes what comes back more than any wording trick.

  5. Send it, then read the answer like an editor

    It will be wrong somewhere. That is expected, not a sign you wrote a bad prompt.

    Compare against the answer key below, tell Gemini what it got wrong in plain language, paste any error, and go again.

A prompt that works 
I use a Google Sheet to track students who each need to complete a requirement. I want a Google Apps Script that decides who needs a reminder and writes the emails for me.

The sheet has one tab called Roster with these columns in this order: Name, Email, Item needed, Status, Due date, Reminders sent, Last reminder. Row 1 is headers. Status is one of Not started, Started, or Complete. Reminders sent is a number. Last reminder is a date or blank.

Here is a sample row so you can see the shape:
Avery Nolan | avery.nolan@example.com | Immunization record | Not started | 2026-08-01 | 0 | (blank)

Go through every row and apply these rules:
- If Status is Complete, skip the row.
- If the due date has not passed, skip the row.
- If past due and Reminders sent is 0, create a first reminder.
- If past due and Reminders sent is 1 and Last reminder was 7 or more days ago, create a second, firmer reminder.
- If past due and Reminders sent is 2 and Last reminder was 7 or more days ago, do not write an email. Add the person to a tab called Needs a call instead.

The emails should be short and warm, address the person by first name, name the specific item they owe, and mention the due date.

Important requirements:
- Create Gmail drafts. Do not send anything.
- After drafting, increase Reminders sent by one and put today's date in Last reminder.
- Add a menu to the spreadsheet called Reminders with one item that runs this.
- When it finishes, show me a popup summarizing how many first reminders, second reminders, and calls came out of the run.
- Add comments explaining what each section does, because I don't write code.
The permission screenOpen this when Google asks for access

Everyone hits this. Nothing has gone wrong - two screens stand between you and a working script.

First screen: "Authorization required"

Cancel is drawn like the safe button. It is not the one you want. Click Review permissions and confirm your EMU account.

Second screen: the permission grant

Check every box, or click Select all, then continue. A partial grant fails later in a way that looks like broken code.

Read that screen before you click. One line says "Read, compose, send, and permanently delete all your email from Gmail." That should get your attention. Three things are true about it:

  • The app asking is yours. The name on that screen says Reminder system because you typed it a minute ago. It is the code you just pasted.
  • The ask is broad because it has to be. Apps Script requests Gmail access at the account level. There is no drafts-only permission to grant.
  • You can take it back. Go to myaccount.google.com/permissions any time and revoke it in about four clicks.
If nothing happensTroubleshooting
  • No new menu? Reload the sheet, and check that you saved in the Apps Script editor.
  • Wrong people getting emails? Check that the column order matches what you told Gemini.
  • Nothing at all, and no error? Almost always a date problem. Sheets dates and script dates do not compare cleanly, and it fails silently.
  • Still stuck? Raise a hand. A flagged failure is worth more to this room than a quiet success.

Step two: write the prompt

Read the notes first - the whole activity turns on what you notice. Then write your instructions and paste the notes underneath in the same message.

This one is all about the rules

No spreadsheet to describe, so almost all the work is in step three: deciding what counts. Read the notes with a pen and argue at your table before you type.

  1. Name the task

    What do you want back? Not "summarize this" - be specific about the shape.

    A table is easier to check than paragraphs. Decide your columns now. Owner and what they agreed to do are obvious; a third column that has saved more meetings than any other is the words from the notes it came from, because it lets you spot a made-up item in two seconds.

    If you are stuck, start hereBelow are the raw notes from a committee meeting. They are messy on purpose. Pull out the action items and give them back to me as a table.

  2. Find the traps before you write a single rule

    Go through the notes and find the places where a fast reader would get it wrong. There are at least five. Some questions to argue about:

    • One item is taken by one person and then handed to someone else a few lines later. Who owns it?
    • Three things need doing and nobody said they would do them. What should the AI do with those?
    • Somebody is named two different ways. How would the AI know that is one person?
    • The room agreed on a couple of things that are settled, not assigned. Are those action items?
    • Somebody mentioned a nice idea that nobody owned and nobody scheduled. Is that an action item?
    • One due date has already gone by, and two others are written in a way you cannot actually pin to a calendar.

    Every one of those needs a sentence in your prompt. If you do not write the rule, the AI decides for you - confidently, and usually wrong.

  3. Write the two rules that matter most

    Everything else is nice. These two are the activity.

    Do not invent an owner. Do not invent a date. Tell it exactly where to put the things it cannot determine - a "Needs an owner" heading, an "(unclear)" tag - so that not knowing has somewhere to go. An AI with no place to put uncertainty will manufacture certainty instead. That is the single most important sentence on this page.

  4. Give it the one fact it cannot get from the notes

    You want overdue items flagged. What does the AI need to know before it can do that?

    It does not know what day the meeting was, and it does not know what day it is now. The notes say "tues 9/8" at the top. Tell it the meeting was Tuesday, September 8, and ask it to flag anything already past. Without that, "already went by" is just words to it.

  5. Ask for the follow-up emails, and say who does not get one

    One short email per owner, listing only that person's items.

    Here is the subtle one: somebody in these notes shows up constantly and owns nothing. They pitched an idea that lost, agreed to review someone else's draft, and asked a question nobody answered. Does a reviewer get an email? Decide, and write it down. If you say nothing, they will get one.

Before you send it

  • The shape you want back, including a column that quotes the notes
  • What to do when nobody took an item
  • What to do when an item changed hands
  • One person, one name
  • Decisions and parked ideas are not action items
  • Do not invent an owner, do not invent a date
  • The date of the meeting
  • Who gets an email and who does not

A prompt that works 
Below are the raw notes from a committee meeting. They are messy on purpose.

Pull out the action items and give them back to me as a table with four columns: Owner, What they agreed to do, Due, and the words from the notes it came from.

These rules matter more than being thorough:
- Do not invent an owner. If nobody in the notes clearly took an item, put it under a separate heading called "Needs an owner" rather than guessing.
- Do not invent a date. If a due date is written in a way you cannot pin down, copy it exactly as it appears and add "(unclear)".
- If someone volunteered for something and it was handed off to someone else later in the same notes, the second person owns it.
- One person may be named more than one way. Use a single name per person.
- A decision is not an action item. Put decisions and anything the room treated as settled under their own heading.
- An idea nobody owns and nobody scheduled is not an action item either. Put those under "Parked."
- The meeting was Tuesday, September 8. Flag any due date that has already passed.

Then write one short follow-up email per owner, listing only that person's items. Warm, three sentences or fewer. Someone who only agreed to review something is not an owner and does not get an email.

Activity 3

Laptop required

Turn a registration list into a caterer's numbers

192 rows. Seven people signed up twice, ten cancelled, fourteen are on the waitlist, and a dozen wrote something in the dietary box that nobody can turn into a meal. The caterer needs a number by Friday.

You are going to build a button that counts it in about four seconds, separates the things that are not actually meal preferences, and refuses to guess at the ones it cannot read. No emails in this one - just the numbers.

Make your copy of the sheet

The rules it has to follow

  1. Cancelled rows drop out entirely, even though they still have data in them.
  2. Waitlist rows get no meal and no headcount. They go on their own tab.
  3. The same email twice is one person. Keep the earliest.
  4. Only people signed up for Lunch eat lunch.
  5. Guests eat what their host eats, and they take up a chair.
  6. Dietary text gets sorted into Standard, Vegetarian, Vegan, or Gluten-free. Blank, "none" and "N/A" all mean Standard. Anything that does not clearly fit gets flagged, not guessed.
  7. An allergy is not a preference. It goes to a human.
  8. Accessibility needs are never dietary needs.

Turning those eight into a prompt an AI can follow is the activity.

Write the prompt

The hardest of the three builds, and the most like real work

You have the rules in plain English above. That is the situation you are in at your desk: you know your job and have to explain it to something that does not.

  1. Say what you need

    You need a set of numbers you can hand to dining services. Describe that, and let the AI propose how.

    If you are stuck, start hereI have a Google Sheet of registrations for a campus open house, and I need to give the caterer a meal count. The list is messy and I keep counting it by hand. What is the best way to handle this?

  2. Describe the sheet, and be careful here

    Nine columns, and two are not simple values. Look at Sessions and Status before you write.

    Sessions holds a comma-separated list, so name the possible values. Status has exactly three states. Guests is a number. Then paste one invented row.

    Nine columns in the wrong order produces a script that reads dietary needs out of the accessibility column - and the output looks completely normal.

  3. Translate the eight rules into instructions

    One at a time. The trick is that plain-English rules leave out what the AI needs most: where does the leftover go?

    "Waitlist rows get no meal" only says what not to do. Say where those people go instead - same for the duplicate, the allergy, the unreadable note, the accessibility need. Without a destination, the AI quietly drops the person or quietly guesses.

    Rule six separates a good prompt from a bad one. Do not just say "sort dietary text into four categories" - add if it does not clearly fit one of the four, do not guess: flag the person and leave them out of the counts. An AI would always rather answer than admit it cannot tell.

  4. Say what it must never do

    This one writes no emails at all, so the guardrail is about your data instead.

    Say "do not write over the Registrations tab." Ask for new tabs. A script that reorganizes your source data in place is a bad afternoon.

  5. Decide what "done" looks like before you look at the output

    You cannot count 192 rows by hand, so you cannot check this the way you checked Activity 1. Check the relationships instead.

    Meals counted plus meals unresolved should equal the lunch headcount exactly. Nobody cancelled or waitlisted should appear anywhere but their own tab. If either fails, something upstream is wrong however sensible the numbers look.

    This is the lesson that transfers. At real scale you never verify the whole output - you build in checks that break loudly, and check those.

Before you send it

  • All nine columns, in order, plus what Sessions and Status can contain
  • One invented sample row
  • A destination for every person who does not fit a clean category
  • The instruction not to guess at unreadable dietary text
  • Guests eat, and guests take up a chair
  • "Do not write over the Registrations tab"

A prompt that works 
I have a Google Sheet of registrations for a campus open house. I want a Google Apps Script that turns it into the numbers I owe the caterer.

The sheet has one tab called Registrations with these columns in this order: Name, Email, Role, Registered on, Sessions, Dietary, Accessibility, Guests, Status. Row 1 is headers. Sessions is a comma-separated list drawn from Morning, Lunch, and Afternoon. Guests is a number. Status is one of Registered, Waitlist, or Cancelled.

Here is a sample row so you can see the shape:
Avery Nolan | avery.nolan@example.com | Staff | 2026-08-01 | Morning, Lunch | Vegetarian | (blank) | 0 | Registered

Work through every row with these rules:
- Ignore Cancelled rows completely.
- Waitlist rows get no meal and no headcount. List them on a tab called Waitlist.
- If the same email appears more than once, keep the earliest registration and put the later one on a tab called Check this.
- Only count a meal for someone whose Sessions include Lunch.
- Each guest eats the same meal as the person who brought them and counts toward the room.
- Dietary was typed by hand and it is inconsistent. Sort each value into exactly one of Standard, Vegetarian, Vegan, or Gluten-free. Blank, "none" and "N/A" all mean Standard. If a value does not clearly belong to one of those four, do not guess: put that person on the Check this tab and leave them out of the counts.
- An allergy is not a meal preference. Anyone with an allergy goes on Check this so I can call catering directly.
- Accessibility needs are never dietary needs. Put them on their own tab called Accessibility, one line per person.

Then build a tab called Counts showing meals by type, a headcount for each session including guests, and how many meals are still unresolved.

Important requirements:
- This script sends no email and creates no drafts. It only reads and counts.
- Do not write over the Registrations tab. Put everything on new tabs.
- When it finishes, show me a popup with the meal counts.
- Add comments explaining what each section does, because I don't write code.

Where it goes wrongSeven ways, and what each one means

None of these announce themselves. The output looks like a tidy table of numbers either way.

  • Zero in the unresolved column. The most dangerous output. The AI decided "same as last time," "ask me" and "TBD" were all Standard rather than admitting it could not tell. Twelve people get the wrong meal and nothing looks broken.
  • Around 119 meals instead of 154. It ignored the Guests column. Thirty-five people arrive expecting lunch and there is no plate.
  • Counted plus unresolved no longer equals 174. Waitlisted people got meals, or the ten cancellations got counted. Invisible in the totals alone.
  • 168 unique people instead of 161. It never deduplicated - seven people counted twice.
  • Allergies folded into the meal counts. Nine people who should have gone to a human got sorted into a category instead.
  • Accessibility needs showing up as diets. "Wheelchair access needed" in the food counts. It read the wrong column, without hesitating.

Same page, same buttons, entirely different agreement behind them. If that corner does not say emu.edu, stop and fix it before you go any further.

Getting in

  1. Go to notebooklm.google.com and sign in with your emu.edu account. Check the corner.
  2. Create a new notebook and add three to five documents. If your files are in Drive, the picker pulls them straight in. Otherwise drag them from your desktop.
  3. Ask it something.

What to put in it

Three to five things your team refers to but nobody can ever find. Process documentation, onboarding material, meeting notes from the past year, a style guide, last year’s report, the FAQ somebody made once and saved somewhere.

Being inside EMU’s agreement is not the same as being the right person to handle something. Personnel files, student records and anything you would not forward to a colleague in another department still stay out. A safer container does not change whose job it is.

Things to try

  • Generate an Audio Overview - start this one first. It takes a few minutes to build, so kick it off and go do something else while it works. Two AI voices discussing your onboarding doc is strange, but it can be a surprisingly useful thing to hear.
  • Ask it something you already know the answer to. If it gets that wrong you have learned something before you trusted it with anything.
  • Ask something you would normally hunt through three documents for.
  • Ask what these documents do not say. The gaps are usually more interesting than the answers.
  • Ask where two of them contradict each other. Most teams have at least one pair that does.
  • Ask what a new hire would find most confusing.
  • Ask for a mind map of how it all fits together.
  • Ask it to draft an FAQ your team could actually use.

Before you trust it

  • The first thing you asked was something you already knew the answer to
  • You clicked a citation and read the source it came from
  • You looked at the date on that source - a confident answer from a document nobody has updated since 2018 is still an answer from 2018
  • If it offered to search the web, you noticed. That is the moment it stops being grounded in your documents and you may not be told twice
  • Nothing you uploaded belongs to somebody else’s job

Take these with you

Three things

  1. Send the shape, not the record

    In the first three activities the AI never saw a real person or a real number. It got column names, invented rows and rules - and the code it wrote ran in your Drive, where the data already lived. Before you paste anything into a public tool at your desk, ask the one question: is this green?

    Activity 4 is the exception that proves it: the same document is fine in one account and not in another. Check the corner, every time.

  2. The method is the part that transfers

    Nobody will hand you a prompt for your workflow. The five moves are what you take back: name it, describe it like they cannot see your screen, write every rule including the do-nothing ones, say what it must never do, then test against an answer you already know.

    Then keep iterating. The first version is never the one that works.
